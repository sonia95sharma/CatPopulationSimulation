How the Simulation Works

A plain-language guide to the model behind the Cat Population Simulator

← Back to the simulator

The simulator tracks a colony of cats forward through time and reports how its size changes under different management strategies. It is a compartmental model: instead of following individual cats, it tracks the numbers of cats in a handful of groups and moves cats between those groups each step according to births, deaths, maturation, and management.

The groups it tracks

Adult cats are split into five groups, because each behaves differently:

  • Intact females — can cycle, mate, and conceive.
  • Spayed females — surgically sterilized; they do not cycle, so they draw no male attention and cannot conceive.
  • AMH-treated females — cannot conceive, but they still cycle and attract males (this is the key difference from spaying).
  • Intact males — can father litters.
  • Neutered males — cannot.

Kittens are held separately in a juvenile stage until they are old enough to breed (see maturation below), reflecting that females reach sexual maturity around {{ v.female_maturity }} months and males around {{ v.male_maturity }} months.

How the model moves through time

The model advances in 6-month steps, so one year is two steps and a 10-year run is 20 steps. In every step it works out the same sequence of events — who mates, how many kittens are born and survive, which adults die, which youngsters grow up, and who moves in or out. Whatever the colony looks like at the end of a step becomes the starting point for the next one, and the running tally is what you see plotted over time.

Cats breed seasonally — roughly January to September in temperate climates — so the model concentrates breeding in that window. Importantly, the number of litters a female has in a year always equals the "litters per year" you set; the season only affects when in the year those litters happen, not how many.

What happens in each step

Think of each 6-month step as running through birth, death, and movement, in this order:

1This year's management is applied. If you're running a yearly program, this round of spaying, AMH, or neutering is applied now to the cats that are still intact. (A one-time program treated cats only at the very start and never again — so its effect gradually fades as those treated cats die and untreated newcomers replace them.)
2Mating & who gets pregnant. This is the heart of the model. Only intact males can father kittens, and each can service just so many matings a day — so male attention is a limited resource. A fertile (intact) female needs some of that attention to conceive, but she is only "available" on a small share of days, because she spends most of the year pregnant or nursing. A spayed female is out of the running entirely — she never cycles. An AMH female, though, keeps cycling and never gets pregnant, so she stays available far more of the time, competing for the same males without ever producing kittens. If the fertile females together want more mating than the males can supply, only a fraction of them conceive. (When males are plentiful — the usual case — everyone who wants to breed does, and AMH and spaying end up looking the same. See the next section.)
3Litters are born. Every female who bred produces her share of the year's litters (about {{ v.mean_litter }} kittens per litter). A queen can't breed endlessly: pregnancy ({{ v.gestation }} days) plus recovery ({{ v.postpartum }} days) plus the time to come back into heat means she can fit at most ~{{ v.max_litters }} litters a year, so the model caps her there.
4Most kittens don't make it. Kitten survival is the model's main brake on growth, and it gets worse as the colony fills up: from about {{ v.base_kmort }}% dying when the colony is small and resources are ample, rising to {{ v.high_kmort }}% as it approaches its carrying capacity. ("Density" just means how full the colony is relative to that maximum.)
5Adults die. A yearly death rate that you set removes a slice of every adult and juvenile group.
6Youngsters grow up. Surviving kittens aren't ready to breed yet. They wait in a juvenile stage and only join the breeding adults about a year after birth, matching when cats actually reach sexual maturity. This delay matters — without it, kittens would breed far too young and the colony would balloon unrealistically.
7Cats come and go. New intact cats arrive (immigration, abandoned pets) and others leave (they wander off, get adopted, or are removed by a shelter). You set these as whole cats per year, split evenly between males and females.
8The ceiling is applied. If you set a maximum colony size, any overflow above it is trimmed away at the end of the step. Turn the maximum off and the colony can grow without limit.

The balance of these forces — how many kittens survive to breed versus how many cats die or leave — is what makes the population on the chart climb, hold steady, or fall.

The male-attention idea (why AMH might differ from spaying)

Each male can service only so many females per day. Spaying removes a female from the mating pool entirely. AMH prevents pregnancy but leaves the female cycling and available — and because she never becomes pregnant, she stays available to mate far more of the time than an intact female (who is pregnant or recovering most of the year). In a trial, treated females mated on roughly 40% of days versus about 15% for intact controls. If males are a limiting resource, those extra AMH matings could tie up male attention and reduce fertile females' breeding — an added effect spaying cannot produce.

Important caveat: this is a hypothesis, not an established population-level effect, and it only matters when intact males are genuinely scarce (uncommon, since a few males can service many females). In most settings the simulator shows AMH and spaying performing almost identically.

Interventions you can combine

  • Spaying, AMH, and neutering can all be applied together in one run.
  • One-time treats the starting population once — coverage then fades as treated cats die and untreated newcomers arrive. Yearly re-treats the intact population every year.
  • Levels can be set as a percentage of the population or as an absolute number of animals (useful when your real-world capacity is, say, 40 cats per year).

Single run vs. many runs

With 1 simulation, the model runs a single, fully predictable trajectory. Set the number of simulations higher and it becomes stochastic: births and deaths are drawn at random each step, with the randomness scaling by population size (small colonies swing more and can die out; large ones are steadier). The app then shows the average trajectory, a 90% confidence band, and the probability of near-eradication (runs ending below one cat).

Cost

Each treatment carries a per-animal cost you set (AMH, spay, neuter). The simulator multiplies these by how many animals were treated over the run to give an estimated program cost, so you can weigh effectiveness against budget.

What the model deliberately keeps simple

  • 6-month steps and a single juvenile stage, rather than tracking exact ages.
  • Adult mortality is one constant rate (not different by sex or reproductive status).
  • Mating is a simplified "attention budget," not mechanistic mate choice.
  • AMH is treated as fully effective and permanent (no waning between doses).
  • No disease, predation, or other outside forces.

This simulator is a simplified model intended for educational and exploratory purposes only. It does not capture all real-world variables and should not be used for policy decisions, medical or veterinary guidance, or other high-stakes planning.
